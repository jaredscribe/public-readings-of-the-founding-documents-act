Rep. Dina Titus and Rep. Mike Carey  
Co-Chairs, Congressional Humanities Caucus  
U.S. House of Representatives  
Washington, DC 20515

Constitution Day is Thursday, September 17, and the House is in session that day. I am
writing to ask the Humanities Caucus to press House leadership to put a reading of the
Constitution on the floor that afternoon, and to make the practice a standing one
thereafter.

The case is a humanities case before it is a procedural one. Since December 2004,
federal law has required every school receiving federal funds to hold an educational
program on the Constitution each September 17, and every federal agency head to give
each employee educational materials that day — Public Law 108-447, Division J, Title I,
Section 111. In more than twenty years, the body that wrote that requirement has never
imposed anything of the kind on itself. Congress directed the nation's teachers to
teach the Constitution, but directed nothing whatever of itself.

A reading is also the cheapest humanities programming available to the federal
government: no appropriation, no grant cycle, no new office — a microphone, willing
readers, and an hour. At a moment when every federal humanities dollar is contested,
the practice that costs nothing is the one worth establishing first, and the one that
makes the argument for the rest.

Three requests:

1. Ask leadership for the floor on the 17th, in both conferences, jointly. A reading
   conducted in turn by Members present displaces no legislative business.
2. Deliver original co-sponsors for the standing rule in January. The House readopts
   its rules each Congress, so permanence means the provision goes into the 120th
   Congress's opening-day rules package. Public-domain model text:  
   https://github.com/jaredscribe/public-readings-of-the-founding-documents-act/blob/main/track-b-chamber-rule-resolution.md
3. If the floor does not happen, hold it yourselves. A caucus reading in a committee
   room or on the Capitol steps on the 17th, open to the press, costs the caucus an
   hour and makes the floor version obviously possible next year. I'll read it on the
   Capitol steps if no one else will set the example of intellectual and moral
   leadership.

Next session, advance a statute. The bill would require that the Constitution Day
educational program schools already owe include a reading of the Constitution aloud to
assembled students, and would ask state library agencies to describe in their plans how
they will make Constitution Day materials available — materials, not a required
reading, and no new appropriation:  
https://github.com/jaredscribe/public-readings-of-the-founding-documents-act/blob/main/track-a-public-readings-of-the-founding-documents-act.md  
A companion bill would carry the practice to the other founding texts, requiring every
federal agency head to hold a reading before noon — of the Constitution on September
17, of the Declaration of Independence on July 4, and on Juneteenth of the Emancipation
Proclamation, General Order No. 3, and the Thirteenth through Fifteenth Amendments:  
https://github.com/jaredscribe/public-readings-of-the-founding-documents-act/blob/main/track-a2-commemorative-days-observance-act.md

Now, lobby the White House for an Executive Order. A letter from the caucus to the
Domestic Policy Council, before this year's Constitution Week proclamation issues,
would cost a staffer an hour and reach a desk at the White House that a private citizen
like myself reaches only by forwarding. Every federal employee should read the
Constitution with his or her coworkers, starting in the Oval Office. The model
executive order:  
https://github.com/jaredscribe/public-readings-of-the-founding-documents-act/blob/main/track-c-executive-order.md

Every text is public domain — free to adopt, amend, rename, or discard, with no
permission needed and no credit owed.

Respectfully,

Jared Essig
